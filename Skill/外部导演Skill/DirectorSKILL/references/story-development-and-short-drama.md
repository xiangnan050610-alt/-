# Story Development & Short-Drama Structure

Supplementary module for Cinematic Director Skill. This file is an additive bridge from writing decisions to the existing directing pipeline; it does not replace any existing template, mode, or production reference.

## 1. Use this module only when needed

Load for: premise/theme/logline development; diagnosing events-without-a-story; protagonist/antagonist goals; inner conflict and character change; scene-level value turns; dialogue subtext and voice; episode/season design; vertical short-drama hooks and cliffhangers; or an AI short-drama workflow whose upstream creative decisions are not yet settled.

Skip it when the user has already locked the story and asks only for a shot list, camera/blocking plan, keyframe, video prompt, model conversion, continuity repair, sound plan, edit, or QC. Those tasks already have routes in the main skill.

## 2. Story premise and controlling idea

Before expanding an idea into scenes, state privately (unless asked to show the analysis):

1. **Protagonist** — who is forced to act, and what makes this person specific?
2. **Concrete external goal** — what observable outcome are they trying to achieve?
3. **Opposing force** — who or what actively prevents that goal, and why does the opposition have a coherent motive?
4. **Stakes and clock** — what changes if the protagonist fails, and what makes action necessary now?
5. **Inner contradiction** — what desire, fear, assumption, or false belief causes the protagonist to make costly choices?
6. **Controlling idea** — what change in value does the ending demonstrate, and through what principal cause?
7. **Story question / logline** — can the central conflict and its unusual hook be communicated clearly in one or two sentences?

A premise is not merely a topic (e.g. “family” or “revenge”). It should imply conflict, causal progression, and a consequential ending. A controlling idea is not a moral pasted into dialogue; the audience should infer it from choices, consequences, and the climax. If the plot's events can be rearranged without changing the protagonist's decisions or outcome, causal structure is probably weak.

### Quick stress tests

- **Specificity:** Can the goal be filmed or otherwise verified, rather than described as “find happiness” or “become stronger”?
- **Opposition:** Does the opposing character have a goal and rationale of their own, rather than functioning as a convenient obstacle?
- **Escalation:** Can the central problem intensify through new costs, reduced options, and irreversible choices?
- **Causality:** Does each major beat arise from a choice, consequence, discovery, or pressure established earlier?
- **Ending:** Does the climax resolve the central dramatic question and reveal a changed or exposed character? Avoid an unrelated accident solving the conflict.
- **Unity:** Do subplots, set pieces, and visual details reinforce, complicate, or deliberately counterpoint the main dramatic question?

If the idea is thin, offer the smallest useful repair: sharpen the goal, strengthen opposition, raise a specific cost, or clarify the protagonist's contradiction. Do not automatically rebuild the whole story.

## 3. Character engine: want, need, belief, pressure, change

For a protagonist and major opponent, distinguish:

- **Want:** the concrete outcome pursued in the plot.
- **Need / missing capacity:** the emotional or ethical capacity the character lacks, avoids, or misunderstands.
- **Belief or misbelief:** the rule the character trusts because it once seemed protective or effective.
- **Pressure strategy:** the tactic used when blocked; it should reveal personality and may worsen the problem.
- **Cost:** what the tactic damages — relationship, status, safety, self-image, time, or a resource.
- **Turn:** the evidence or consequence that makes the old strategy harder to maintain.
- **Change:** the final choice that demonstrates growth, refusal to change, or tragic entrenchment.

Do not reduce a character to a diagnosis or a generic “flaw.” The belief must be connected to behavior and the story's particular pressure. The opponent also needs an intelligible objective, values, tactics, and limits. A strong conflict often comes from two people pursuing understandable goals through incompatible methods.

For an ensemble, give each important character a distinct want, worldview, verbal rhythm, information advantage, and relationship to the central theme. Do not make every character sound like the writer.

## 4. Scene craft: objective, resistance, turn, consequence

A scene should change at least one meaningful variable: power, knowledge, trust, danger, desire, status, commitment, or available options. For each scene, determine:

1. Entry state — what each participant wants and what they know.
2. Tactic — what they do to get it (ask, conceal, bargain, threaten, seduce, delay, test, flee, expose, etc.).
3. Resistance — who or what blocks the tactic.
4. Turn — new information, a failed tactic, a choice, or a reversal changes the balance.
5. Exit state — what is now different, and what question pulls the audience forward?

A scene may be quiet and still have a strong turn. Conversely, shouting, action, or a dramatic camera move does not itself create a story beat. If the entry and exit states are identical, cut, compress, combine, or redesign the scene unless its deliberate function is atmosphere, suspense, contrast, or necessary orientation.

Use action and behavior before explanatory dialogue. Make the audience infer intent from choices, timing, avoidance, physical business, and what a character refuses to answer.

## 5. Dialogue, subtext, and distinct voices

For every line, ask: What does the speaker want from the listener right now? What are they unwilling to say directly? What tactic is this line using? What changes after it is spoken?

- Prefer playable intentions over abstract emotion labels: “deflects blame with a joke” is more actionable than “feels awkward.”
- Build subtext from a gap between literal words and immediate objective; do not make every line cryptic.
- Differentiate voices through sentence length, directness, vocabulary, rhythm, metaphor habits, confidence, status, and what each person notices or avoids.
- Let interruptions, pauses, unfinished sentences, silence, overlap, and physical action carry meaning only when motivated by the relationship or moment.
- Avoid exposition that characters already know unless there is a credible reason to remind, persuade, manipulate, or test another character.
- Check speakability: a line must fit the allotted time at a natural pace, including pauses and reactions. For a short clip, prefer a few brief exchanges to a speech that forces rushed delivery.
- In AI-generated dialogue, separate spoken words from performance directions, speaker identity, timing, and sound cues using the existing sound/dialogue reference. Do not promise reliable lip-sync unless the target workflow supports it.

## 6. Episode, season, and short-form serial structure

Use only if the project is episodic or serial. First identify the episode's own dramatic question and resolution, then its relationship to the season-long engine.

- **Episode spine:** setup/teaser → escalating attempts → midpoint shift or new information → pressure/cost increase → climax/choice → aftermath or end button. Treat this as a diagnostic pattern, not a mandatory act count.
- **A/B/C strands:** A-story carries the main episode conflict; B-story should echo, challenge, or complicate its theme; C-story/runner is used only when it creates contrast, payoff, or useful rhythm. Avoid parallel plots that never affect each other or the episode's meaning.
- **Act-out / cliffhanger:** end a section on a consequential choice, reversal, reveal, danger, unanswered question, or changed relationship. The strongest cliffhanger changes the audience's understanding or the character's options; withholding arbitrary information alone is weak.
- **Season engine:** define what naturally generates the next episode, what can change each week, what remains invariant, and how the season escalates toward a larger irreversible decision. A premise that only supports one reveal may not sustain a season.
- **Continuity:** track which information each character knows, what promises/debts/conflicts remain open, and how the prior episode's ending constrains the next one. Keep this distinct from the visual continuity bible, which remains in its existing file.

For vertical short drama, add a platform-aware rhythm without forcing a formula:

1. Establish a legible situation and a specific disturbance quickly.
2. Give the protagonist a clear immediate goal or vulnerability.
3. Escalate with visible choices and concrete consequences, not only repeated accusations or reaction shots.
4. Place reveals where they change the meaning of what came before.
5. End on a turn that creates a meaningful next question: a decision, discovery, threat, reversal, arrival, or relationship shift.
6. Ensure the next episode can actually pay off the hook; do not promise a twist that the story cannot support.

Duration and platform requirements are constraints, not excuses to cram many major actions into one generated shot. After the dramatic beat map is sound, hand off to the existing shot-plan, video-prompt, sound, editing, and QC modules.

## 7. AI short-drama production handoff and decision ownership

Before prompting, record the current approved state:

- source script / version and requested deliverable;
- user-locked dialogue, shot order, timing, blocking, templates, and visual anchors;
- creative decisions still open versus decisions already approved;
- required assets, sound, continuity, and acceptance criteria;
- target duration, aspect ratio, target model if known, and cost/permission boundaries where relevant.

**Respect locked decisions.** If the user provides or repeatedly adjusts a template, dialogue, timing, shot order, or staging, treat it as authoritative. Improve only the requested scope. Do not add or delete shots, rewrite dialogue, reorder beats, or replace a template silently.

**Do not confuse text deliverables with media execution.** A request for a story idea, script, storyboard, prompt, or Skill test does not automatically authorize actual video generation. When an actual video is requested, use the existing production workflow to execute, review, repair, and deliver the requested artifact. Never claim a video was generated merely because a prompt or storyboard exists.

**Keep the deliverable in scope.** If the user asks for one prompt, return the prompt. If they ask for a script, write the script. Use deeper analysis internally and expose it only when requested or when a concise note is necessary to explain a material assumption.

## 9. Completion check

Before moving into directing, confirm that the relevant story decisions are stable enough for the requested task: the scene/episode goal is clear; the opposing force is active; beats have causal links; character choices drive turns; dialogue fits time and voice; the ending resolves or deliberately reframes the dramatic question; and user-approved decisions remain intact. Only check applicable items, and do not print this checklist unless asked.
